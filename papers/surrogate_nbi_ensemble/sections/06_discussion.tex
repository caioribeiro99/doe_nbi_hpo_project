\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

One claim organizes the results: on the classifier-weight simplex the mixture-design response surface is a good
\emph{description} of ensemble performance and an unreliable \emph{optimization substrate}, and the dominant error is
not inside the surface but in the points that build the geometry of the search.

\subsection{Why anchors dominate}
\label{sec:disc:anchors}

NBI derives its whole search geometry from the payoff matrix --- utopia point, normalization, convex hull of
individual minima, quasi-normal directions --- so every subproblem in \cref{eq:nbi} is posed relative to the
individual optima. An anchor error is therefore global rather than local: it relocates the CHIM, redirects every
quasi-normal and rescales the objective space of all 66 subproblems, while an interior error of the same size perturbs
single solutions and leaves the frame intact.

The asymmetry predicts what we observe. Replacing surrogate anchors with real ones, surfaces held fixed, improves both
primary endpoints in $30/30$ partitions on the two datasets whose ROC-AUC surfaces almost never pass the gate, and in
$23$--$24/30$ on Porto Seguro and $24/30$ on UCI credit, with hypervolume gains up to $+0.18$; removing the surrogate
entirely, once anchors are correct, adds much less on three of the four datasets. The fix is also cheap: real anchors
are single-objective optima a practitioner already computes, and on the convex log-loss that is one SLSQP call, so
NBI-B costs essentially what NBI-A costs while repairing most of the gap to the metamodel-free arm.

BNP Paribas shows it concretely: where the parsimony rule selects a quadratic ROC-AUC surface, the surrogate maximum
sits at an interior mixture instead of the gradient-boosting vertex, so the CHIM is built from a false utopia point
and the hypervolume ratio falls from about $0.91$ to $0.04$--$0.12$ --- a geometric failure, not a numerical one,
since the solver certifies these subproblems, and invisible to any diagnostic computed on the surface alone.

\subsection{Why the reliability gate does not catch it}
\label{sec:disc:gate}

The gate asks whether a surface predicts unseen compositions well on average; anchor placement depends on where the
surface puts its \emph{argmax}, a far more local property. A surface can be globally accurate and still misplace an
extremum, or globally poor with its argmax on the right vertex.

On BNP Paribas they diverge actively: the parsimony rule selects the quadratic order precisely when it predicts
held-out points better, so the collapse partitions have higher external $R^2$ (median $0.46$ against $0.08$) and pass
the gate more often ($3$ of $7$ against $2$ of $23$) than the partitions where the surrogate anchor is right. A linear
Scheff\'e surface has its maximum at a vertex by construction, so the \emph{less} flexible model is accidentally
protected while the more flexible one may place an interior maximum the data do not support.

Hence a division of labour: the gate is worth its 100 extra evaluations for separating unusable surfaces from
excellent ones, but only the cheaper anchor check that distinguishes NBI-B from NBI-A removes the failure instead of
diagnosing it.

\subsection{Smooth surrogates against a rank statistic}
\label{sec:disc:smooth}

Two of the three objectives behave as the framework's engineering origins assume: log-loss is convex and smooth in
$\wvec$, the Brier score exactly quadratic ($R^2_{\mathrm{ext}} = 1.000$ in all 120 replications). The ROC-AUC is not:
a rank statistic, constant wherever the induced ordering does not change and kinked between orderings, which a
low-order polynomial smooths across instead of representing. Hence the Santander ROC-AUC surface reaches a Spearman
correlation of $0.830$ with a negative external $R^2$ --- ordering broadly right, values wrong.

The resulting bias is one-directional: on 20 of 40 dataset-pair cells (\cref{sec:results:coefficients}) the surface
predicts an edge blend superior to both pure components where the real objective delivers none, and in no case does it
miss a real one. A quadratic fitted over an edge with one weak endpoint absorbs that curvature into $\beta_{ij}$ and
overshoots its maximum --- the same over-prediction of interior optima that displaces surrogate anchors off vertices.

In the originating application the responses are physical measurements, smooth in the process factors, where a
quadratic is a defensible local model; discrimination metrics are not, and no experimental design repairs a
misspecified model class. Where the objective \emph{is} smooth in the weights the machinery works as advertised:
Santander's log-loss surface passes the gate in every partition.

\subsection{Non-smoothness also constrains the optimizer}
\label{sec:disc:solver}

The same property constrains the metamodel-free arm: SLSQP cannot certify optimality on a piecewise-constant
objective, whose gradient is zero almost everywhere, so NBI-C accepts a feasible iterate with equality residual below
$10^{-3}$, a weaker guarantee than the certification NBI-A and NBI-B obtain on smooth polynomials. Success rates are
therefore not on a common scale across arms, and we do not read the difference as evidence about surrogate quality.

UCI credit shows why that care is needed: its $+0.277$ hypervolume gain for NBI-C over NBI-B, at Holm-corrected
$p = 0.010$, looks like the strongest evidence here for the metamodel-free arm, yet UCI credit's surfaces pass the
gate in every partition, so surrogate error cannot explain it: NBI-B certifies a median of 29 of its 66 subproblems,
the gap correlates with the converged count at Spearman $-0.92$, and in the three partitions where NBI-B converges
fully it disappears.

\subsection{Compute against fidelity}
\label{sec:disc:compute}

Metamodel-free NBI reaches median hypervolume ratios of $0.976$--$0.989$ and is best or tied-best on the primary
endpoints, so it is a defensible default, but it spends about $4.2\times10^{5}$ real evaluations per replication
against none for the surrogate arms, at 3 to 73 times the wall-clock cost. That spend is strongly conditional: on UCI
credit the threefold premium buys the largest gain, while on Santander a seventy-three-fold premium buys $+0.008$
hypervolume on a dataset whose log-loss surface is already exact.

Its affordability is specific to this problem: the ensemble is linear in $\wvec$ and the base-model probabilities are
cached, so one evaluation is a matrix--vector product plus a sort, $0.37$ to $2.13$~ms here. Where each evaluation
needs a model refit the ordering reverses and the surrogate is the only viable route --- the regime the framework was
designed for. The recommendation is therefore conditional on evaluation cost: cheap evaluations should be used
directly, at least for the anchors; expensive ones argue for an externally validated surrogate, anchored on whatever
real evaluations are affordable.

\subsection{Response surfaces as descriptions rather than optimizers}
\label{sec:disc:description}

The surfaces themselves are reproducible --- over 30 resampled partitions the four leading interaction coefficients
keep their sign in all eight dataset--response cells, the leading term is identical at $R = 10$ and $R = 30$ in all
eight, and coefficient means move by at most 6\% (\cref{sec:results:coefficients}) --- and as a description of which
vertices are weak and which regions flat the model is interpretable in a way a black-box optimizer is not. What does
not survive is the step from that description to inference about blends: the classical synergism criterion
$\beta_{ij} > |\beta_i - \beta_j|$ is correct as stated and guards against reading the sign of $\beta_{ij}$ alone, but
it is computed \emph{from the surface}, so on these ensembles it inherits the surface's systematic over-prediction of
interior optima, and a candidate pair must be confirmed on the real objective --- one matrix product.

\subsection{Deployment cost is not the cost that was optimized}
\label{sec:disc:cost}

Every method here minimized the continuous relaxation $\sum_i w_i c_i$, since a step cost is not differentiable and
cannot enter a gradient-based formulation; deployment pays the step cost. The two are not monotonically related inside
the simplex, and the consequence is not cosmetic: the hypervolume-best method changes between the definitions in $10$,
$24$, $8$ and $20$ of 30 partitions, and several paired conclusions reverse, including the anchor effect on UCI
credit.

The mechanism is that a linear cost gives no incentive to zero a component: a weight of $0.01$ on a model costing
$200$~ms adds $2$~ms under the relaxation and $200$~ms in deployment. Methods optimizing the relaxation drift into the
interior and miss the cheap low-support solutions --- pure vertices and two-model faces --- that the unoptimized
66-run design contains by construction, which is why that design wins the support-cost hypervolume outright in 13 of
30 BNP Paribas partitions.

Related work prices deployment as a support cost over a fixed selected set or as a per-example cascade, and the
sparsity-inducing formulations that do use a continuous relaxation of a support count rarely audit whether the
relaxation and the support agree. Where that question has been examined in a related setting, the answer went the
other way: for budgeted learning with feature re-use, a support-cost programme is solved exactly by its linear
relaxation \citep{nan2015budgeted} --- a guarantee our setting does not inherit. Report which cost model is used; zero
weights below the activity threshold and re-evaluate, since an inert weight just above $\varepsilon$ produced a
spurious $250$~ms difference in our own analysis; and optimize over supports explicitly where deployment cost is the
real objective.

\subsection{What the framework's transfer teaches}
\label{sec:disc:transfer}

The pipeline transfers where the response is smooth in the mixture components, a condition identifiable in advance.
Where the ROC-AUC surface is adequate too --- UCI credit, which passes in $30/30$ --- surrogate NBI with real anchors
is within $0.03$ hypervolume of the metamodel-free arm, and random scalarization on those surfaces is statistically
indistinguishable from it: there the inherited machinery is the efficient choice. It does not transfer unexamined to a
rank-statistic response, to anchors that are vertices of the feasible region rather than interior points, or to an
objective that is a step function of the support --- three generic properties that break model class, geometry and
objective definition respectively.

What we would carry forward is not a method but a checklist: validate the surface on compositions it has not seen;
compare each surrogate argmax against the real optimum and prefer the real one; revalidate every candidate on the true
objective before scoring; build a reference whose sampled core no method under test can influence; and state which
cost you are paying. Each of them changed a conclusion that would otherwise have been reported.
