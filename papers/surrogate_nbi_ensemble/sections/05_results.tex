\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Results are organized by research question, with per-claim provenance in the supplementary claims map. Indicators
use the weighted cost of \cref{eq:weightedcost} unless stated otherwise.

One fact frames the rest: weighting helps, but modestly. The log-loss optimum improves out-of-fold ROC-AUC over the
best single model by $0.0037$, $0.0056$, $0.0060$ and $0.0007$ --- one to two orders of magnitude more than the
differences between the multiobjective methods studied here.

Surrogate adequacy depends on dataset and response alike (\cref{tab:gate}, \cref{fig:gate}). The ROC-AUC surface
passes the gate in $0/30$ partitions on Santander, $5/30$ on BNP Paribas, $16/30$ on Porto Seguro and $30/30$ on UCI
credit; the log-loss surface passes in $30/30$, $3/30$, $12/30$ and $30/30$. Santander is sharpest: on the same
partition its log-loss surface is essentially exact (median $\rext = 0.971$) while its ROC-AUC surface has
\emph{negative} external $\rext$ (median $-0.333$) --- worse than predicting the mean --- even though its external
rank correlation is a respectable $0.830$. Porto Seguro sits on the threshold for both responses and 30 partitions do
not resolve it either way --- a property of the problem, not a sample-size deficiency.

The failures are misspecification, not extrapolation: the excess of predicted over observed range on the validation
points is approximately zero everywhere, so the surrogate is not predicting outside its fitted region. As a
control, the Brier score ---
genuinely quadratic in $\wvec$ --- is reproduced with $\rext = 1.000$ in all 120 replications, so design and fitting
are sound and the failure is specific to the response (\cref{fig:surrogate}).

\subsection{RQ2: Surface stability and what the coefficients mean}
\label{sec:results:coefficients}

The surfaces are stable across resampling: the four largest interaction coefficients keep their sign in all eight
dataset--response cells, the largest-magnitude term is the same at $R = 10$ and $R = 30$ in all eight and is the
per-partition largest in $30/30$ partitions in seven of them (on Porto Seguro's ROC-AUC surface two
$k$-nearest-neighbour pairs alternate, $19/30$ against $11/30$), and between-partition coefficients of variation for
the leading terms lie between $0.011$ and $0.17$.

Interpreting those coefficients requires the classical criterion
\citep{scheffe1958mixtures,cornell2002mixtures} stated correctly. The edge profile
$\hat y(t) = \beta_j + (\beta_i - \beta_j)t + \beta_{ij}t(1-t)$ implied by
\cref{eq:scheffequad} has an interior optimum above both pure components if and only if
\begin{equation}
\beta_{ij} \;>\; \lvert \beta_i - \beta_j \rvert
\label{eq:edgecond}
\end{equation}
(derivation in supplementary Section~S3), which already guards against the naive reading: a large $\beta_{ij}$ on an
edge joining two very unequal vertices need not beat the better one. Our finding is not that this criterion is
unknown, but that \emph{the fitted surfaces pass it and are still wrong}.

For the largest-$\beta$ pair of every dataset \cref{eq:edgecond} holds in all thirty partitions (\cref{tab:edge}), yet
on the real out-of-fold objectives the predicted superior blend materializes on one dataset only. The real 50/50 blend
is \emph{worse} than its better member in $30/30$ partitions on Santander ($-0.0050$ ROC-AUC), BNP Paribas ($-0.0180$)
and UCI credit ($-0.0201$), and beats both members only on Porto Seguro ($+0.0098$, $30/30$); the untouched holdout
repeats this ($0/30$, $0/30$, $29/30$, $0/30$), and optimal rather than 50/50 weighting does not rescue it
(supplementary Section~S3). Across all ten pairs criterion and reality agree on 6, 4, 7 and 3; all 20 disagreements
are the surface predicting a blend the real objectives do not deliver, and none is a real one missed.

\input{tables/tab07_edge_condition}

The mechanism is visible in \cref{fig:beta}: the largest interaction attaches to the \emph{weakest} vertex ---
$k$-nearest neighbours on Santander and Porto Seguro, Gaussian naive Bayes on BNP Paribas and UCI credit --- and
tracks the pair's vertex-quality gap (0.163, 0.145, 0.022 and 0.107). Consistently, the pairs whose real blend does
beat its better member in at least 80\% of partitions are different,
smaller-$\beta$ pairs on every dataset except Porto Seguro (supplementary Section~S3).

The criterion is correct and should be applied through \cref{eq:edgecond} rather than by reading the sign of
$\beta_{ij}$, but on these ensembles it inherits the misspecification of the surface it is computed from and must be
confirmed on the real objective --- one matrix product per pair --- before a pair is called complementary. Log-loss
interactions are uniformly negative and uninformative, since log-loss is convex in $p_{\wvec}$ and Jensen's inequality
forces blending to look favourable; only the ROC-AUC signs are interpretable, and those are the ones the blend test
contradicts.

\subsection{RQ3 and RQ4: Surrogate versus real anchors}
\label{sec:results:anchors}

\input{tables/tab03_paired_primary}

Replacing the surrogate-derived anchors with anchors computed from the real single-objective optima, changing nothing
else, improves both primary endpoints in all 30 partitions on Santander ($\Delta\hv = +0.179$, 95\% CI $[0.148,
0.210]$; Holm-corrected $p = 0.004$) and on BNP Paribas ($\Delta\hv$ median $+0.059$). On the other two the
improvement is large but not unanimous, and the endpoints differ: Porto Seguro $23/30$ partitions on $\igdp$ and
$24/30$ on hypervolume ($\Delta\hv = +0.085$, $[0.037, 0.131]$), UCI credit $24/30$ on $\igdp$ and 24 wins with one
tie and five losses on hypervolume ($+0.060$, $[0.034, 0.083]$). The rank-biserial effect size is $1.00$ on the two
unanimous datasets and $0.65$--$0.75$ on the others, and the Wilson interval for a $30/30$ win fraction is $[0.89,
1.00]$ (\cref{tab:paired}, \cref{fig:paired}, \cref{fig:fronts}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig04_paired_deltas.pdf}
\caption{Paired differences on the two primary endpoints, one point per outer partition, red bar at the median; the
IGD$^+$ axis is symmetric-logarithmic. The BNP Paribas NBI-B $-$ NBI-A distribution is visibly bimodal: small positive
differences plus a tail of seven partitions where NBI-A collapses.}
\label{fig:paired}
\end{figure}

Three qualifications bound this. First, the corrected test reaches significance only on Santander (and, for NBI-C
versus NBI-B, on UCI credit); on BNP Paribas the effect is unanimous in sign yet the corrected $p$ is $0.66$, because
the differences are bimodal --- the mean $+0.279$ is over four times the median $+0.059$ --- so by the rule fixed in
advance the median with its bootstrap interval $[0.051, 0.070]$, the win fraction and the rank-biserial correlation
are the evidence. Second, the Porto Seguro and UCI credit win rates are driven by the twenty partitions added in the
extension (hypervolume $18/20$ and $19/20$ against $6/10$ and $5/10$ with one tie in the first ten; on UCI credit the
batch difference is itself significant under an identical protocol, Fisher $p = 0.009$): the effect is real in
direction, but its magnitude varies enough between draws of ten partitions that ten were not enough to see it. Third,
it depends on the cost definition: under the support cost Santander is unchanged ($30/30$) and Porto Seguro stays
positive on $\igdp$ ($29/30$), but BNP Paribas weakens to $19$--$20/30$ and UCI credit \emph{reverses}, NBI-B being
worse in $26$--$27/30$ partitions (full support-cost repetition in supplementary Table~S1).

\paragraph{What goes wrong when anchors come from the surrogate.} NBI-A's BNP Paribas hypervolume ratio splits into 21
partitions at $0.89$--$0.93$, two intermediate and seven collapsing to $0.04$--$0.12$, and the seven are exactly those
in which the parsimony rule selects the \emph{quadratic} ROC-AUC order ($7/7$ against $0/22$ of the linear-selected
ones). A quadratic surface moves the surrogate AUC anchor off the gradient-boosting vertex to an interior mixture the
empirical reference dominates; in six of the seven that mixture carries $k$-nearest neighbours at 16--31~ms per
1{,}000 rows, so anchor cost tracks the collapse (Spearman $-0.69$), but one partition collapses with a cheap anchor,
making cost a marker rather than the mechanism.

The consequence for the gate is uncomfortable. Because the quadratic order is selected precisely when it predicts
unseen points better, the collapsed partitions have \emph{higher} external $\rext$ (median 0.46 against 0.08) and pass
the gate \emph{more} often (3 of 7 against 2 of 23) than those where NBI-A works: a gate on surface quality does not
protect against this failure, while real anchors do, NBI-B reaching $0.935$--$0.996$ in all thirty partitions. It also
settles how the gate-conditional analysis must be read --- the apparently larger anchor benefit where the gate passes
on BNP Paribas ($+0.64$ against $+0.21$) is NBI-A's collapse restated, since three of the five gate-passing partitions
are collapse partitions, and is not evidence that real anchors add more when the surface is reliable.

\subsection{RQ5: Surrogate NBI against metamodel-free NBI}
\label{sec:results:metamodelfree}

NBI-C gives the best or tied-best approximation among the surrogate-derived methods on every dataset
(\cref{sec:results:nsga2} shows an external evolutionary optimizer does
better still). NBI-C's median hypervolume ratio is $0.989$, $0.983$, $0.982$ and $0.976$. It exceeds $0.97$ in every
partition of Santander and BNP Paribas but only in $24/30$ and $25/30$ partitions of Porto Seguro and UCI credit;
uniformly, it exceeds $0.95$ in all thirty partitions of three datasets and in $26/30$ on Porto Seguro (worst
partition $0.877$). Against NBI-B it wins on hypervolume in $28$, $24$, $28$ and $30$ of 30 partitions, the corrected
test reaching significance on Santander ($p = 0.003$) and UCI credit ($p = 0.010$).

Three caveats bound this. On BNP Paribas the arms genuinely split: NBI-C covers more volume ($24/30$) while NBI-B is
closer to the reference in $\igdp$ ($24/30$, $\Delta = -0.003$). The large UCI credit gap is not a surrogate-accuracy
effect --- those surfaces pass the gate in every partition --- but a convergence effect: NBI-B certifies a median of
29 of its 66 subproblems, indicators use converged candidates only, and the gap correlates with that count at Spearman
$-0.92$; retaining NBI-B's unconverged iterates preserves the direction ($30/30$) but cuts the mean gap from $+0.277$
to $+0.108$, and in the three partitions where NBI-B converged fully the gap is at most $0.01$. Neither observation
supports a claim that interior surrogate error dominates once anchors are right. Third, the premium is large and
uneven (\cref{tab:compute}): NBI-C consumes about $4.2\times10^{5}$ real objective evaluations per replication where
the surrogate arms consume none, at 73, 40, 45 and 3 times the wall-clock time of NBI-B --- smallest on UCI credit,
where it buys the largest gain, and 73-fold on Santander for $+0.008$ hypervolume on a dataset whose log-loss surface
is already exact. Metamodel-free NBI is the safe default, justified where the surfaces or the anchors fail, not
uniformly preferable.

\input{tables/tab05_compute}

\paragraph{Cheap comparators.} Random weighted scalarization is strong where the surfaces are reliable --- it ties
NBI-C on UCI credit (differences within $\pm 0.002$, win fractions $16/30$
and $18/30$, corrected $p = 1.00$), matches it on Santander hypervolume ($0.978$ against $0.989$) and beats it on
Santander $\igdp$ in $20/30$ --- and collapses where they are not, to a median of one front point and hypervolume
ratio $0.474$ on BNP Paribas. The comparison is confounded and we say so: NBI-C optimizes exact objectives while
scalarization optimizes surrogates, so its BNP Paribas and Porto Seguro advantage cannot be attributed to the NBI
construction rather than to the objective source. On identical surfaces NBI-B beats scalarization $30/30$ on BNP
Paribas and $25/30$ on Porto Seguro but loses $29/30$ on UCI credit.

The 66-point random Dirichlet sample loses to every method everywhere --- its hypervolume ratio is exactly zero in
$10/30$, $21/30$ and $25/30$ partitions on Santander, BNP Paribas and Porto Seguro, because uniform compositions cost
8 to 20 times more than the reference front, and the unoptimized 66-run mixture design also beats it $30/30$
everywhere --- but it is matched to the NBI sets only in the number of candidates returned, so this is a floor check
and no efficiency claim is made from it.

One negative result bears on the classical motivation for NBI recalled in \cref{sec:method:nbi}: the even spread of
subproblems over the CHIM does not survive revalidation on the real objectives, under any spacing definition we
computed. NBI-C's size-matched spacing percentile is $0.99$, $0.54$, $0.96$ and $0.41$, against $1.00$, $1.00$, $1.00$
and $0.93$ for the random Dirichlet sample; \cref{sec:results:nsga2} shows this is a property of the construction, not
of the objective geometry.

\subsection{RQ5b: An evaluation-matched evolutionary baseline}
\label{sec:results:nsga2}

Everything above compares methods within one methodological family; the external question is whether a canonical
evolutionary optimizer, given the same objectives and budget, behaves differently. We ran NSGA-II \citep{deb2002nsga2}
in pymoo \citep{blank2020pymoo} on exactly the three real out-of-fold objectives NBI-C optimizes, with a population of
66 matching the 66 NBI directions, library-default variation operators, a deterministic simplex repair, and a
termination budget set \emph{per replication} to the real objective evaluations NBI-C consumed there; the
configuration was fixed and committed before any run, so no operator setting was chosen by inspecting performance
(supplementary Section~S13). Across the 120 replications the realized budget ratio is $0.999996$ overall and lies in
$[0.99992, 1.00010]$ per run, so the comparison is genuinely evaluation-matched. It is not time-matched, and the
wall-clock ratio is reported separately. Because the empirical reference is augmented with candidate sets, adding a
method changes it; we therefore score twice, against a \emph{sample-core} reference containing the search output of no
optimizer, and against a \emph{common augmented union} that includes NSGA-II and against which every method's
indicators are recomputed.

\input{tables/tab08_nsga2}

The answer is unambiguous and we state it plainly: \textbf{NSGA-II attains a better approximation than NBI-C on both
primary endpoints, on all four datasets, under both reference definitions} (\cref{tab:nsga2}; the sample-core
repetition is supplementary Table~S2). All 16 dataset $\times$ endpoint $\times$ reference cells favour NSGA-II, with
rank-biserial correlations between $+0.87$ and $+1.00$. The corrected test reaches significance on Santander, BNP
Paribas and UCI credit hypervolume; on Porto Seguro the paired differences are heavy-tailed (mean $+0.021$ against
median $+0.011$), so by the rule fixed in advance the median, the win fraction and the effect size are the evidence
there, and all three agree.

Three qualifications matter. The margins are small in absolute terms: median hypervolume-ratio gains of $+0.006$ to
$+0.013$, on a scale where NBI-C already reaches $0.977$--$0.990$. NSGA-II costs about $2.2\times$ the wall clock of
NBI-C at equal evaluation count, because a generation of 66 evaluations amortizes fixed costs less well than NBI's
batched subproblem solves. And NSGA-II contributes 8--29\% of the augmented reference front, so like NBI-B and NBI-C
it is partly graded against its own output there --- which is why the sample-core reference, where it contributes
nothing, is reported alongside and gives the same answer.

This also settles what the surrogate-only comparison could not: whether NBI's lost spacing advantage reflects the
objective geometry or the CHIM construction. NSGA-II's crowding-distance selection produces markedly more evenly
spaced revalidated fronts on every dataset --- median spacing coefficient of variation $0.74$, $0.62$, $0.60$ and
$0.65$ against NBI-C's $2.28$, $0.88$, $1.56$ and $1.92$ --- so even spacing here is attainable and CHIM-based
construction does not deliver it. Scored post hoc under the support cost, which NSGA-II never optimized, the advantage
largely transfers (supplementary Table~S2), most strongly on BNP Paribas; the one reversal is Santander $\igdp$, where
NBI-C is marginally closer in 17 of 30 partitions.

This bounds one claim --- NBI-C is best or tied-best \emph{among the surrogate-derived methods}, not among
multiobjective optimizers generally --- and leaves untouched every conclusion about the surrogate pipeline itself
(reliability, anchor misplacement, the gate's blind spot, the coefficient result, the cost-definition result), because
NSGA-II is not part of that pipeline and was never proposed as a component of it. A conventional optimizer on the same
objectives behaves differently, modestly and consistently, and a practitioner whose evaluations are cheap enough to
run either should prefer it.

\subsection{RQ6: The actual multiobjective conflict}
\label{sec:results:conflict}

In objective space the conflict between discrimination and calibration is almost degenerate: the direct ROC-AUC
optimum exceeds the SLSQP log-loss optimum by $0.00068$, $0.00080$, $0.00006$ and $0.00034$ ROC-AUC, with bootstrap
interval half-widths below $0.00004$ --- 5 to 100 times less than the gain from ensembling at all. Two qualifications
prevent the stronger statement that the problems are the same: the optima differ in weight space ($L_1$ distances
$0.35$, $0.38$, $0.06$ and $0.20$, the Porto Seguro pair practically coinciding), and Santander pays $0.0032$ of
log-loss for its $0.00068$ of ROC-AUC, so the practitioner gets a different \emph{model} even where the objective
values are nearly indistinguishable. An apparent $+254$~ms per 1{,}000 rows deployment-cost gap on Santander did not
survive checking and we do not claim it: it is an artifact of the activity threshold in \cref{eq:supportcost}, a
near-threshold $k$-nearest-neighbour weight that is AUC-inert, and an AUC-equivalent blend without $k$-nearest
neighbours costs about $+4$~ms per 1{,}000 rows (supplementary Section~S9).

\input{tables/tab06_holdout_cost}

The two cost definitions disagree often enough to change conclusions (\cref{fig:cost}, \cref{tab:holdout_cost}): the
hypervolume-best method changes between them in $10/30$ partitions on Santander, $24/30$ on BNP Paribas, $8/30$ on
Porto Seguro and $20/30$ on UCI credit. NBI-C remains best under both on Santander and Porto Seguro ($26/30$ and
$20/30$); on UCI credit random scalarization takes the support-cost hypervolume in $22/30$, and several paired
readings reverse outright (\cref{sec:results:anchors}, \cref{sec:results:metamodelfree}).

The mechanism, where it can be isolated, is that a method optimizing $\sum_i w_i c_i$ has no incentive to zero out a
component. The raw 66-run design occupies exactly the cheap supports the NBI sets miss and can win outright under a
step cost --- as it does in $13/30$ BNP Paribas partitions.

One earlier reading did not survive scrutiny and we correct it here: the apparent bimodality of NBI-C's BNP Paribas
support hypervolume is a normalization artifact, not a regime. The per-partition normalization box has a cost bound
near 17~ms per 1{,}000 rows in the 13 partitions where no $k$-nearest-neighbour-bearing point survives on the support
reference front and near 160--167 in the other 17 --- a tenfold rescaling of one axis --- while NBI-C's fronts are the
same in both groups, and under a common bound the split disappears. We therefore claim only the qualitative statement:
NBI sets optimized on the linear cost omit the cheapest supports the raw design contains, and the penalty's size
depends on the chosen cost range.

\subsection{RQ8: Transfer to the untouched holdout}
\label{sec:results:holdout}

In level, yes. The mean holdout-minus-out-of-fold ROC-AUC of every knee-selected solution lies within $\pm 0.005$ as a
point estimate, and the shift equals the weight-averaged shift of the base models on the same partition (correlation
$0.97$--$1.00$): a partition and refitting effect shared with the constituent models, not selection optimism.

In ranking, it depends on the dataset. The out-of-fold-best of the four knee picks is also holdout-best in $30/30$
partitions on Santander, $15/30$ on BNP Paribas, $23/30$ on Porto Seguro and $30/30$ on UCI credit, and the twenty
added partitions reproduce this pattern exactly ($20/20$, $10/20$, $15/20$, $20/20$). The BNP Paribas figure measures
how close the four candidates are, not a failure of selection: their median out-of-fold gap is $0.0005$ against a
holdout noise standard deviation of about $0.0012$, and when the two disagree the holdout regret is at most $0.0022$
ROC-AUC. These rates are specific to the selection rule and cost definition --- under the support cost they become
$28$, $28$, $17$ and $16$ of 30 --- and are not dataset properties.

\subsection{RQ9: Did ten replications suffice?}
\label{sec:results:r30}

\begin{figure}[t]

Tripling the number of replications changed no directional conclusion (\cref{tab:r10r30}, \cref{fig:r10r30}): the $R =
10$ estimate lies inside the $R = 30$ bootstrap interval in 9 of 12 hypervolume cells, 7 of 12 $\igdp$ cells and all 8
gate frequencies, and the sign of the median paired effect is identical in all 32 primary cells. What the extension
delivered is a change of status for four findings --- the Porto Seguro and UCI credit anchor effect went from an
undecidable $6/10$ and $5/10$ to $24/30$ with intervals excluding zero; the BNP Paribas anchor collapse became a
quantified rate ($7/30$) with an identified trigger (quadratic order selection); the NBI-C versus NBI-B split on BNP
Paribas resolved into a genuine hypervolume/$\igdp$ trade-off; and the BNP Paribas support-cost bimodality and the
Santander deployment-cost gap were exposed as artifacts. The criterion is itself lenient, because partitions 0--9 are
one third of the $R = 30$ sample: against the twenty new partitions alone only 6 of 12 hypervolume and 5 of 12 $\igdp$
cells agree, and the Porto Seguro and UCI credit anchor win rates differ between batches under an identical protocol.
That disagreement is the magnitude of partition sensitivity the replication was designed to measure (supplementary
Section~S11).
