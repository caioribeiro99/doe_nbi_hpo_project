\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section states the optimization problem, the mixture-design and response-surface machinery inherited from the
predecessor framework, and the three Normal Boundary Intersection (NBI) variants that separate the effect of surrogate
fidelity from the effect of anchor construction. \Cref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline and marks each element as
inherited from the predecessor framework, adapted for compositional decision variables, or new in this study.

\subsection{The ensemble-weighting problem is a mixture problem}
\label{sec:method:problem}

Let $M$ probabilistic base classifiers produce, for a sample $x$, the probability estimates
$p_1(x),\dots,p_M(x) \in [0,1]$. A weighted soft-voting ensemble predicts
\begin{equation}
p_{\wvec}(x) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i\, p_i(x) \;=\; \mathbf{P}\wvec ,
\label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{P} \in [0,1]^{n \times M}$ collects the base-model probabilities for $n$ samples. For
$p_{\wvec}(x)$ to remain a probability for every input, the weights must satisfy
\begin{equation}
w_i \ge 0 \quad (i = 1,\dots,M), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i = 1 ,
\label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
that is, $\wvec \in \simplex$, the unit simplex in $\mathbb{R}^{M}$. \Cref{eq:simplex} is exactly the canonical
constraint set of a \emph{mixture experiment}: the components are the classifiers, the pure vertex $w_i = 1$ is the
single model $i$ used alone, and the centroid $\wvec = (1/M)\mathbf{1}$ is uniform soft voting. The experimental
region is a simplex, not a box, so factorial and central-composite designs and ordinary polynomial response surfaces
with an intercept are not applicable: the sum-to-one constraint makes the design matrix singular. The appropriate
formalism is the mixture design with a Scheff\'e canonical polynomial, and this correspondence --- rather than any
performance argument --- is the reason the framework transfers at all.

Three objectives are considered, all functions of $\wvec$ through \cref{eq:ensemble}:
\begin{align}
f_1(\wvec) &= -\operatorname{AUC}\!\left(y, \mathbf{P}\wvec\right) && \text{(discrimination, maximized)},\\
f_2(\wvec) &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[-y_k \log p_{\wvec}(x_k) - (1-y_k)\log\!\big(1-p_{\wvec}(x_k)\big)\Big] && \text{(calibration, minimized)},\\
f_3(\wvec) &= c(\wvec) && \text{(inference cost, minimized)}.
\end{align}
The three differ qualitatively, and that heterogeneity is the point of the study. The log-loss $f_2$ is convex and
smooth in $\wvec$. The area under the ROC curve is a rank statistic: $f_1$ is piecewise constant in $\wvec$, with
gradients that are zero almost everywhere and undefined on the boundaries between orderings, so it is not merely
non-convex but non-smooth in a way that defeats gradient-based certification. The cost $f_3$ is discussed next.

\subsection{Two cost models: a relaxation and a deployment cost}
\label{sec:method:cost}

Let $c_i$ be the measured inference cost of model $i$ in milliseconds per 1{,}000 predictions, including its
preprocessing. Deployment cost is a step function of the weight vector: a model that receives \emph{any} non-negligible
weight must be loaded, fed and executed, and one that receives none costs nothing. This gives the
\emph{support cost}
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{sup}}(\wvec) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{M} c_i \,\mathbf{1}\!\left[w_i > \varepsilon\right], \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-3},
\label{eq:supportcost}
\end{equation}
which is discontinuous and therefore unusable as an objective for a gradient-based multiobjective method. Every
optimizer in this study instead sees the continuous relaxation
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{w}}(\wvec) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i c_i ,
\label{eq:weightedcost}
\end{equation}
which is the natural linear mixture response and coincides with $c_{\mathrm{sup}}$ at the vertices. The two are not
monotone transformations of each other on the interior: a weight vector that spreads a small amount of mass over an
expensive model is cheap under \cref{eq:weightedcost} and expensive under \cref{eq:supportcost}. We therefore treat
\cref{eq:weightedcost} as the optimization objective and \cref{eq:supportcost} as a second, operationally meaningful
scoring of the \emph{same} solutions, and we report every comparison under both. To our knowledge this contrast has not
previously been drawn for weighted ensembles; \cref{sec:results:cost} shows that it changes which method wins.

\subsection{Mixture design and Scheff\'e surrogates}
\label{sec:method:scheffe}

\paragraph{Design.} With $M = 5$ the design is a fixed 66-run mixture design, evaluated once per replication on the
cached out-of-fold probabilities. It is the union of the $\{5,3\}$ and $\{5,2\}$ simplex lattices, the overall
centroid, the 5 axial points, the 5 quaternary (four-component) centroids and the 10 midpoints between the overall
centroid and the ternary centroids. This composition guarantees that the 5 pure vertices (each single model), all 10
binary edges, the ternary and quaternary faces and uniform voting are design points, so the surrogate is fitted with
support on every face of the simplex that a sparse ensemble could occupy.

\paragraph{Models.} For a response $y$ the Scheff\'e canonical polynomials of order $1$, $2$ and the special cubic are
\begin{align}
\text{linear:}\quad & \hat y = \sum_i \beta_i w_i, \\
\text{quadratic:}\quad & \hat y = \sum_i \beta_i w_i + \sum_{i<j} \beta_{ij} w_i w_j, \label{eq:scheffequad}\\
\text{special cubic:}\quad & \hat y = \sum_i \beta_i w_i + \sum_{i<j} \beta_{ij} w_i w_j + \sum_{i<j<k} \beta_{ijk} w_i w_j w_k,
\end{align}
fitted by ordinary least squares without an intercept and without pure quadratic terms, since $\sum_i w_i = 1$ makes
them redundant. No term elimination is performed: the canonical form is kept intact so that $\beta_i$ is the predicted
response at vertex $i$ and $\beta_{ij}$ measures the departure of the $i$--$j$ edge from linear blending.

\paragraph{External validation and the reliability gate.} The lineage reports in-sample fit statistics. Here each
replication additionally evaluates 100 compositions drawn from $\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$ (60 points) and
$\mathrm{Dirichlet}(0.5\cdot\mathbf{1})$ (40 points) that are \emph{not} in the design and are never used for fitting.
On these points we compute the external coefficient of determination $\rext$ and the Spearman rank correlation
$\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}$ between predicted and observed response. Two decisions follow.

\emph{Order selection} uses a parsimony rule: the lowest order whose external RMSE is within 10\% of the best external
RMSE among the three orders is selected. Selection is therefore driven by out-of-design predictive accuracy, not by
in-sample fit.

\emph{Admissibility} uses a pre-registered \emph{reliability gate}: a surface is declared reliable only if
\begin{equation}
\rext \ge 0.5 \quad\text{and}\quad \rho_{\mathrm{ext}} \ge 0.9 .
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
The thresholds were fixed before the replicated study and were never adjusted. The gate is a hypothesis under test in
this work, not a filter applied to the results: surrogate-based optimization is run and reported \emph{regardless} of
the gate outcome, precisely so that the gate's predictive value can be measured (\cref{sec:results:gate}).

\subsection{Normal Boundary Intersection on the simplex}
\label{sec:method:nbi}

NBI \citep{das1998nbi} generates an even spread of Pareto points by intersecting the boundary of the attainable set
with normals to the convex hull of individual minima. For $m$ objectives $F = (F_1,\dots,F_m)$ let
$\wvec^{*(k)}$ minimize $F_k$; the payoff matrix $\Phi$ has columns $F(\wvec^{*(k)})$, its diagonal is the utopia
point $F^{\mathrm{u}}$, and objectives are normalized by the utopia and pseudo-nadir points extracted from $\Phi$.
Writing $\bar\Phi$ for the normalized payoff matrix and $\hat{n} = -\bar\Phi\mathbf{1}$ for the quasi-normal direction,
each $\beta$ on the unit simplex of \emph{scalarization} weights defines the subproblem
\begin{equation}
\max_{\wvec,\,t} \; t \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \bar\Phi\beta + t\,\hat{n} = \bar F(\wvec), \quad \wvec \in \simplex .
\label{eq:nbi}
\end{equation}
Two implementation points matter here. First, the simplex constraint is eliminated by optimizing over $M-1$ free
variables with $w_M = 1 - \sum_{i<M} w_i$, and a projection step returns the nearest feasible composition when an
iterate leaves the simplex; without it, subproblems near a vertex can terminate outside the feasible set. Second, for
vertex values of $\beta$ the subproblem solution is the corresponding anchor, which is returned directly as a warm
start rather than re-derived. We solve 66 subproblems per replication, using the same $\beta$ lattice for all variants
so that the three arms are compared at identical points of the CHIM.

\paragraph{The three variants.} The variants differ in exactly two binary choices --- where the objectives come from,
and where the anchors come from --- and in nothing else:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Variant & Objectives in \cref{eq:nbi} & Anchors / payoff matrix & Real objective evaluations \\
\midrule
NBI-A & Scheff\'e surfaces & optima of the Scheff\'e surfaces & 0 \\
NBI-B & Scheff\'e surfaces & real out-of-fold single-objective optima & 0 (anchors reused) \\
NBI-C & real out-of-fold objectives & real out-of-fold single-objective optima & $\approx 4.2\times10^{5}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

NBI-A is the construction used in the predecessor framework and in the forecast-combination precedent: everything,
including the payoff matrix, is derived from the metamodel. NBI-B changes one thing only --- the anchors are the real
optima found by the single-objective references of \cref{sec:method:refs} --- so the difference between A and B
isolates the effect of \emph{anchor misplacement} while holding the surrogate surfaces fixed. NBI-C removes the
metamodel entirely and runs the same NBI on the exact cached out-of-fold objectives, so the difference between B and C
isolates the residual effect of \emph{surrogate error in the interior} once the anchors are correct. Because $f_1$ is
piecewise constant, NBI-C uses finite-difference steps of $10^{-3}$, multistart from points drawn on the simplex, and
accepts an iterate whose equality residual is below $10^{-3}$ even when the solver cannot certify optimality; NBI-A and
NBI-B, which optimize smooth polynomials, require full SLSQP certification. This asymmetry is a consequence of the
objective, not a tuning choice, and it is reported with the results.

\subsection{References, comparators and revalidation}
\label{sec:method:refs}

\paragraph{Single-objective references.} For each replication we compute the best single model, uniform voting,
logistic stacking, the SLSQP optimum of the log-loss (convex, so this is the global optimum), a direct
derivative-free search for the ROC-AUC optimum over $4\times10^{4}$ sampled compositions plus local refinement, and the
optima of the two Scheff\'e surfaces. The log-loss and AUC optima double as the real anchors of NBI-B and NBI-C. We
emphasize that SLSQP on the convex log-loss remains the correct tool for the single-objective problem; NBI is not
proposed as a replacement for it.

\paragraph{Comparators.} Two cheap alternatives are run on every replication: random weighted scalarization, which
draws 66 scalarization weight vectors from $\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$ and minimizes the corresponding weighted
sum of the \emph{same} Scheff\'e surfaces with the same real anchors used by NBI-B; and a 66-point random
$\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$ sample of the weight simplex. The second is matched to the NBI sets only in the
\emph{number of returned candidates} and is not compute-matched; it functions as a floor check on the geometry of the
reference front, not as an efficiency baseline, and is reported as such throughout.

\paragraph{Revalidation.} Every candidate produced by any method --- NBI-A, NBI-B, NBI-C, both comparators, the 66
design runs and the single-objective references --- is re-evaluated on the exact out-of-fold objectives before any
indicator is computed, and non-dominated filtering is applied to those revalidated values. No result in this paper is
read off a surrogate.

\subsection{Empirical Pareto reference and quality indicators}
\label{sec:method:reference}

Comparing approximation sets requires a reference. Since the true Pareto set of \cref{eq:ensemble} over $\simplex$ is
unknown, each replication constructs an \emph{empirical Pareto reference}: at least $10^{5}$ compositions sampled from
$\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$ and $\mathrm{Dirichlet}(0.3\cdot\mathbf{1})$ in equal parts, augmented with the
simplex lattice, all vertices and edges, and an $\varepsilon$-constraint sweep with 40 caps solved by SLSQP, all
evaluated on the real objectives and filtered to the non-dominated set. Convergence is checked by an independent
sample of $2\times10^{4}$ further points: if these displace more than 5\% of the current front, the reference is
enlarged and the check repeated, up to three rounds. The final reference is the non-dominated union of this sample
with every candidate set produced in the replication, which makes it at least as good as any method being scored.

We call this an empirical reference and never a true Pareto front. Two consequences are stated with the results: it
is a sample-based upper bound on attainable quality, and because candidate sets contribute to it, a method that
contributes many points is partly graded against itself.

Approximation sets are scored on min--max normalized objectives by generational distance, inverted generational
distance, $\igdp$ \citep{ishibuchi2015igdplus}, Schott spacing, the exact hypervolume with reference point $1.1$ on
each axis, expressed as a ratio to the reference hypervolume, coverage, and the joint non-dominated fraction. $\igdp$
and the hypervolume ratio are pre-registered as the two primary endpoints; the others are descriptive.
