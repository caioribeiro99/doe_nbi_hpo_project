\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The ensemble-weighting problem is a mixture problem}
\label{sec:method:problem}

Let $M$ probabilistic base classifiers produce, for a sample $x$, probability estimates
$p_1(x),\dots,p_M(x)$ in $[0,1]$. A weighted soft-voting ensemble predicts
\begin{equation}
p_{\wvec}(x) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i\, p_i(x) \;=\; \mathbf{P}\wvec ,
\label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{P} \in [0,1]^{n \times M}$ collects the base-model probabilities for $n$ samples. For
$p_{\wvec}(x)$ to remain a probability for every input, the weights must satisfy
\begin{equation}
w_i \ge 0 \quad (i = 1,\dots,M), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i = 1 ,
\label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
that is, $\wvec \in \simplex$, the unit simplex in $\mathbb{R}^{M}$. \Cref{eq:simplex} is the canonical
constraint set of a \emph{mixture experiment}: the components are the classifiers, the pure vertex $w_i = 1$ is the
single model $i$ used alone, and the centroid is uniform soft voting. The experimental region is a simplex, not a box,
so factorial designs and ordinary polynomials with an intercept are inapplicable --- the sum-to-one constraint makes
the design matrix singular --- and the appropriate formalism is the mixture design with a Scheff\'e canonical
polynomial. This correspondence, rather than any performance argument, is why the framework transfers at all.

Three objectives are considered, all functions of $\wvec$ through \cref{eq:ensemble}:
\begin{align}
f_1(\wvec) &= -\operatorname{AUC}\!\left(y, \mathbf{P}\wvec\right) && \text{(discrimination, maximized)},\\
f_2(\wvec) &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[-y_k \log p_{\wvec}(x_k) - (1-y_k)\log\!\big(1-p_{\wvec}(x_k)\big)\Big] && \text{(calibration, minimized)},\\
f_3(\wvec) &= c(\wvec) && \text{(inference cost, minimized)}.
\end{align}
The three differ qualitatively, and that heterogeneity is the point of the study. The log-loss $f_2$ is convex and
smooth in $\wvec$. The area under the ROC curve is a rank statistic: $f_1$ is piecewise constant in $\wvec$, with
gradients that are zero almost everywhere and undefined on the boundaries between orderings, so it is not merely
non-convex but non-smooth in a way that defeats gradient-based certification. The cost $f_3$ is discussed next.

\subsection{Two cost models: a relaxation and a deployment cost}
\label{sec:method:cost}

Let $c_i$ be the measured inference cost of model $i$ in milliseconds per 1{,}000 predictions, including its
preprocessing. Deployment cost is a step function of the weight vector: a model that receives \emph{any} non-negligible
weight must be loaded, fed and executed, and one that receives none costs nothing. This gives the
\emph{support cost}
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{sup}}(\wvec) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{M} c_i \,\mathbf{1}\!\left[w_i > \varepsilon\right], \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-3},
\label{eq:supportcost}
\end{equation}
which is discontinuous and therefore unusable by a gradient-based method. Every optimizer here instead sees the
continuous relaxation
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{w}}(\wvec) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i c_i ,
\label{eq:weightedcost}
\end{equation}
the natural linear mixture response, which coincides with $c_{\mathrm{sup}}$ at the vertices but not on the
interior: spreading a little mass over an expensive model is cheap under \cref{eq:weightedcost} and fully priced under
\cref{eq:supportcost}. We therefore optimize \cref{eq:weightedcost} and additionally score the \emph{same} solutions
under \cref{eq:supportcost}, reporting every comparison under both. \Cref{sec:results:cost} shows that the choice
changes which method wins.

\subsection{Mixture design and Scheff\'e surrogates}
\label{sec:method:scheffe}

\paragraph{Design.} With $M = 5$ the design is a fixed 66-run mixture design: the union of the $\{5,3\}$ and
$\{5,2\}$ simplex lattices, the overall centroid, the 5 axial points, the 5 quaternary centroids and the 10 midpoints
between the overall centroid and the ternary centroids. It therefore places design points on the 5 pure vertices, all
10 binary edges, the ternary and quaternary faces and uniform voting, giving the surrogate support on every face a
sparse ensemble could occupy.

\paragraph{Models.} For a response $y$ the Scheff\'e canonical polynomials of order $1$, $2$ and the special cubic are
\begin{align}
\text{linear:}\quad & \hat y = \sum_i \beta_i w_i, \\
\text{quadratic:}\quad & \hat y = \sum_i \beta_i w_i + \sum_{i<j} \beta_{ij} w_i w_j, \label{eq:scheffequad}\\
\text{special cubic:}\quad & \hat y = \sum_i \beta_i w_i + \sum_{i<j} \beta_{ij} w_i w_j + \sum_{i<j<k} \beta_{ijk} w_i w_j w_k,
\end{align}
fitted by ordinary least squares without an intercept and without pure quadratic terms, since $\sum_i w_i = 1$ makes
them redundant. No term elimination is performed: the canonical form is kept intact so that $\beta_i$ is the predicted
response at vertex $i$ and $\beta_{ij}$ measures the departure of the $i$--$j$ edge from linear blending.

\paragraph{External validation and the reliability gate.} Where the lineage reports in-sample fit statistics, each
replication here additionally evaluates 100 compositions drawn from $\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$ (60) and
$\mathrm{Dirichlet}(0.5\cdot\mathbf{1})$ (40) that are not in the design and never used for fitting, giving an
external $\rext$ and Spearman $\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}$. \emph{Order selection} then uses a parsimony rule --- the lowest
order whose external RMSE is within 10\% of the best --- so it is driven by out-of-design accuracy rather than
in-sample fit. \emph{Admissibility} uses a \emph{reliability gate} whose thresholds were fixed and committed to version control
before the replicated benchmark was run: a surface is reliable only if
\begin{equation}
\rext \ge 0.5 \quad\text{and}\quad \rho_{\mathrm{ext}} \ge 0.9 .
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
They were never adjusted afterwards. We describe this as pre-specification rather than preregistration: there is no
external registry entry, and the evidence is the version-controlled commit history. The gate is a hypothesis under
test in this work, not a filter applied to the results: surrogate-based optimization is run and reported \emph{regardless} of
the gate outcome, precisely so that the gate's predictive value can be measured (\cref{sec:results:gate}).

\subsection{Normal Boundary Intersection on the simplex}
\label{sec:method:nbi}

NBI \citep{das1998nbi} was introduced to obtain a distributed representation of the Pareto boundary, by advancing
from the convex hull of individual minima along quasi-normal directions. Whether that distribution survives the map
into revalidated objective space is one of the questions this paper tests, and \cref{sec:results:metamodelfree}
reports that in our setting it does not. For $m$ objectives $F = (F_1,\dots,F_m)$ let
$\wvec^{*(k)}$ minimize $F_k$; the payoff matrix $\Phi$ has columns $F(\wvec^{*(k)})$, its diagonal is the utopia
point $F^{\mathrm{u}}$, and objectives are normalized by the utopia and pseudo-nadir points extracted from $\Phi$.
Writing $\bar\Phi$ for the normalized payoff matrix and $\hat{n} = -\bar\Phi\mathbf{1}$ for the quasi-normal direction,
each $\beta$ on the unit simplex of \emph{scalarization} weights defines the subproblem
\begin{equation}
\max_{\wvec,\,t} \; t \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \bar\Phi\beta + t\,\hat{n} = \bar F(\wvec), \quad \wvec \in \simplex .
\label{eq:nbi}
\end{equation}
Two implementation points matter. The simplex constraint is eliminated by optimizing over $M-1$ free variables with
$w_M = 1 - \sum_{i<M} w_i$, with a deterministic feasibility-recovery step for iterates that leave the simplex: the
free variables are clipped to $[0,1]$ and, if they still sum above one, renormalized onto the face $w_M = 0$. This is
a documented repair, not a Euclidean projection onto the simplex, and a candidate recovered this way is flagged
infeasible and excluded from the certified set. Without it, subproblems near a vertex terminate outside the feasible
region altogether. And for vertex values of $\beta$ the
solution is the corresponding anchor, returned directly rather than re-derived. We solve 66 subproblems per
replication on the same $\beta$ lattice for all variants, so the arms are compared at identical points of the CHIM.

\paragraph{The three variants.} The variants are defined by two methodological factors --- the source of the
objectives and the source of the anchors:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Variant & Objectives in \cref{eq:nbi} & Anchors / payoff matrix & Real objective evaluations \\
\midrule
NBI-A & Scheff\'e surfaces & optima of the Scheff\'e surfaces & 0 \\
NBI-B & Scheff\'e surfaces & real out-of-fold optima & 0 (anchors reused) \\
NBI-C & real out-of-fold objectives & real out-of-fold optima & $\approx 4.2\times10^{5}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

NBI-A is the construction used throughout the lineage: everything, including the payoff matrix, comes from the
metamodel. NBI-B changes one thing --- the anchors are the real optima of \cref{sec:method:refs} --- so A versus B
isolates \emph{anchor misplacement} with the surfaces held fixed. NBI-C removes the metamodel entirely, so B versus C
isolates the residual effect of \emph{surrogate error in the interior} once anchors are correct. Because $f_1$ is
piecewise constant, NBI-C uses finite-difference steps of $10^{-3}$, multistart on the simplex, and accepts an iterate
whose equality residual is below $10^{-3}$ even without certified optimality, whereas NBI-A and NBI-B optimize smooth
polynomials and require full SLSQP certification. This asymmetry follows from the objective, not from tuning, and is reported with the results. It also bounds the
causal reading: A versus B isolates the anchor source with the surrogate objectives and the solver held fixed, and is
a clean contrast; B versus C tests removal of the surrogate once real anchors are supplied, but is not a perfectly
identical numerical experiment, because changing the objective from a smooth polynomial to a piecewise-constant rank
statistic necessarily changes solver semantics. Solver outcomes are therefore reported separately for each arm.

\subsection{References, comparators and revalidation}
\label{sec:method:refs}

\paragraph{Single-objective references.} Each replication computes the best single model, uniform voting, logistic
stacking, the SLSQP log-loss optimum (convex, hence global), a derivative-free ROC-AUC optimum over $4\times10^{4}$
sampled compositions with local refinement, and the optima of the two Scheff\'e surfaces. The log-loss and AUC optima
double as the real anchors of NBI-B and NBI-C. SLSQP on the convex log-loss remains the correct tool for the
single-objective problem; NBI is not proposed as a replacement for it.

\paragraph{Comparators.} Two cheap alternatives are run on every replication: random weighted scalarization, which
draws 66 scalarization weight vectors from $\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$ and minimizes the corresponding weighted
sum of the \emph{same} Scheff\'e surfaces with the same real anchors used by NBI-B; and a 66-point random
$\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$ sample of the weight simplex. The second is matched to the NBI sets only in the
\emph{number of returned candidates} and is not compute-matched; it functions as a floor check on the geometry of the
reference front, not as an efficiency baseline, and is reported as such throughout.

\paragraph{Revalidation.} Every candidate from every method --- both NBI variants on surrogates, NBI-C, both
comparators, the 66 design runs and the references --- is re-evaluated on the exact out-of-fold objectives before any
indicator is computed, with non-dominated filtering applied to those revalidated values. No result in this paper is
read off a surrogate.

\subsection{Empirical Pareto reference and quality indicators}
\label{sec:method:reference}

Comparing approximation sets requires a reference, and the true Pareto set of \cref{eq:ensemble} over $\simplex$ is
unknown. Each replication therefore constructs an \emph{empirical Pareto reference}: at least $10^{5}$ compositions
from $\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$ and $\mathrm{Dirichlet}(0.3\cdot\mathbf{1})$ in equal parts, augmented with the
simplex lattice, all vertices and edges, and a 40-cap $\varepsilon$-constraint sweep, evaluated on the real objectives
and filtered to the non-dominated set. An independent sample of $2\times10^{4}$ points checks convergence: if it
displaces more than 5\% of the front, the reference is enlarged and rechecked, up to three rounds. The final reference
is the non-dominated union of this sample with every candidate set of the replication, making it at least as good as
any method scored against it.

We call this an empirical reference, never a true Pareto front: it is a sample-based upper bound, and because
candidate sets contribute to it, a method contributing many points is partly graded against itself.

Approximation sets are scored on min--max normalized objectives by generational distance, inverted generational
distance, $\igdp$ \citep{ishibuchi2015modified}, Schott spacing, the exact hypervolume with reference point $1.1$ on
each axis, expressed as a ratio to the reference hypervolume, coverage, and the joint non-dominated fraction. $\igdp$
and the hypervolume ratio were fixed in advance as the two primary endpoints; the others are descriptive.
